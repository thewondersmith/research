Dead Air: Are 911 Recording Systems the Next Target?

Rae Baker· November 2026· Critical Infrastructure, OSINT, Credential Exposure
Methodology note: Everything here came from certificate transparency logs, Shodan, public documentation, and commercial breach databases. No login attempts were made, no systems were accessed, no credentials were tested. Passive reconnaissance only.

With the current state of cyber events, I started thinking about what would happen if 911 systems were taken offline. What I found: the infrastructure to do it is already sitting on the public internet, with critical vulnerabilities that give an attacker a clear path in.

The platform in question is a 911 call recording appliance deployed in Public Safety Answering Points across the country. Honestly, I didn't know it existed until I did some light Googling and then some certificate lookups, and what came back was a full customer list from certificate transparency logs, decade-old CVEs still running in production, default credentials printed in a publicly accessible manual, breached partner access in stealer malware archives, and a path to full appliance compromise on at least one deployed system.

Let's break down how I got there.

crt.sh handed me a customer list

The goal is security, but often the side effect is that every subdomain an organization has ever provisioned is permanently, publicly searchable by anyone with a browser. I searched for [product-domain] across two [Vendor] domains to see what comes back.

What Shodan told me about their versions

Shodan indexes internet-connected devices by what they say when you connect: software versions, response headers, banner text. I looked up the IPs from the [Product] subdomains. The version numbers map directly to known CVEs. All [Product] appliances run lighttpd. The version tells you how recently it was updated.

System lighttpd CVEs Notes
saas-naa.[product-domain].cloud 1.4.79 0 CVEs [Vendor]'s own SaaS — 403, no login exposed
putnamco.nexlog.cloud 1.4.67 5 CVEs Login page on HTTPS; jQuery UI 1.10.1 (2013)
intrado911demo.poc.nexlogdx.host 1.4.67 5 CVEs Login served over plain HTTP
eschat01.nexlog.host 1.4.35 CVE-2019-11072 CVSS 9.8 — unauthenticated RCE. Not "access recordings." Own the box.

[Vendor] runs the latest lighttpd with zero CVEs on their own systems. Every customer system I checked is running an unpatched version. [Vendor] appears to be patching but they can't force their customers to.

Every customer [Product] login page is running jQuery UI 1.10.1. Released January 2013. That's the same JavaScript library version that was current during Obama's second inauguration. It's sitting on the login form of a 911 recording system in 2026, with five unpatched CVEs including cross-site scripting on the login form itself.

The vendor product manual reveals technology details

[Vendor] publishes the full [Product] Series User Manual, 480 pages, as a downloadable PDF on their public website. I searched it for the word "default." It appeared three times in the same context.

The manual documents two sets of factory default credentials, printed in plain text, available to anyone who downloads the PDF. It also documents a factory-installed OpenVPN connection pre-configured at manufacture: "While it can be altered or overwritten, it is not recommended. Altering this connection would prevent [Vendor]'s remote assistance if ever needed." [Product] User Manual, P/N #141338 — publicly available, [Vendor] website
[Product] desktop client — authentication screen

Every [Product] ships with a persistent VPN back to [Vendor]. Customers are actively discouraged from removing it. And if default credentials were never changed, that's enough to get in without it.

What leaves a 911 center after installation

Following the certificates on [product-domain].host further opened up the backend. Every licensed [Product] continuously pushes data to monitor.[product-domain].host via NMS heartbeat: call statistics, system alarms, database stats. Every unit phones home to license.[product-domain].host for license verification. And poc.[product-domain].host showed integration with Prepared 911, now Axon, which covers 1,000+ PSAPs and roughly 90 million people.

What leaves a 911 center after a [Product] is installed

A single appliance in a 911 center is simultaneously maintaining a persistent VPN to [Vendor] HQ, pushing call statistics to a central monitoring console, checking its license on a remote server, potentially uploading recordings to [Vendor]'s AI SaaS, and feeding data into an Axon-owned AI platform. The 911 center administrators see a login page but everything else is running in the background.

But what about exposed credentials?

The source file names showed infostealer log archives, FileGrabber output from compromised machines, and Telegram URL:Login:Password channels. This isn't a static breach from a single incident, but rather data exfiltrated by malware from endpoints that were actively logged into [Vendor]'s systems.

The two access levels that matter most were [Vendor]'s partner portal and their software download repository. Partner credentials are worth more than customer credentials to me here. Authorized [Vendor] resellers install and service [Product] across multiple PSAPs. One compromised partner account is a lateral movement path into every 911 center they manage. Those credentials were sitting in ALIEN TXTBASE and Naz.API, indexed across multiple Telegram trading channels.

When I ran the organization's domain through DeHashed, 11 named staff appeared in the DemandScience breach: full names, direct phone numbers, organizational email addresses. That's the low-consequence hit. Naz.API was more interesting.

Naz.API is compiled from infostealer malware logs like Redline and Vidar. It doesn't mean a third party got breached, it means malware was running on an endpoint, and it captured webmail credentials. Then that user likely rotated the password but the malware was still there so it captured the new one too.

One 911 center, start to finish

Northwest Central Dispatch handles 911 for 12 Chicago suburbs: Arlington Heights, Buffalo Grove, Des Plaines, Elk Grove Village, Hanover Park, Hoffman Estates, Mount Prospect, Palatine, Rolling Meadows, Schaumburg, Streamwood, and Wheeling.

Access Appliance admin console — plain HTTP, no authentication, all connected [Product] clients visible
  1. crt.sh: A [product-domain].cloud subdomain confirms this is an [Vendor] customer. Login page on the public internet.
  2. Shodan: lighttpd 1.4.67, jQuery UI 1.10.1 from 2013, five unpatched XSS CVEs. HTTPS login page, publicly accessible.
  3. Vendor PDF: Factory default credentials documented in plain text in a freely downloadable PDF on [Vendor]'s own website. If those defaults were never changed, the investigation ends here.
  4. Naz.API: A staff account from this organization appears in an infostealer compilation, with webmail credentials captured before and after a password rotation. Sequential, plaintext, same endpoint, two captures.
  5. Webmail to [Product]: Webmail access is a password reset path into the [product] dashboard. From there the goal isn't passive surveillance, it's the appliance itself. Combined with CVE-2019-11072 on a 1.4.35 host, that could be unauthenticated remote code execution to potentially crash the recording service or wipe the call database... even take the system offline entirely. During an active incident (a mass shooting, a multi-agency response), that's a blackout of recorded evidence for every unit and call.

The credential pivot requires no active exploitation at all, it's basically super low cost and low effort. The CVE context is also free and sitting on 1.4.35 hosts, unauthenticated, CVSS 9.8, for anyone who wants to skip credentials entirely and go straight to code execution on the appliance. The exposure exists because these systems were designed to be internet-accessible, default credentials are documented in a publicly downloadable manual, and the credential hygiene at a 911 center is not unique.

This is an availability problem

A 911 recording system that goes dark for an extended period of time, especially during a mass casualty event, could be devastating. Ransomware operators have already figured this out and 911 centers have been targeted specifically because operational downtime creates pressure to pay the ransom. The credential and CVE surface mapped here is exactly what that playbook requires.

[Vendor] likely maintains their own infrastructure well. Their systems are patched, their login is blocked, and their lighttpd is current. That doesn't appear to extend to their customers. Customer-facing systems run libraries from 2013, default credentials are documented in a freely downloadable manual, and a persistent vendor VPN ships pre-installed with explicit guidance not to remove it.

For any organization running internet-accessible systems, the first question isn't what your known vulnerabilities are, it's what's already visible from outside. Certificate Transparency logs permanently document every subdomain you've ever provisioned. Shodan indexes every exposed service. Infostealer compilations are searchable by domain. An attack surface assessment that stops at CVEs misses the layer where most of this research starts. The power of open-source exposure isn't any single finding, it's correlation. A publicly listed subdomain plus an outdated software version plus a domain credential in a stealer log isn't three separate data points. It's a complete access chain.

For PSAPs (Public Safety Answering Points): run your domains through HaveIBeenPwned. Check DeHashed for your government email domain. If your credentials are in a stealer log, your data isn't yours anymore.
